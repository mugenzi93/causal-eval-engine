Cross-Method Conflicts

IV VS AIPW VS PSM VS RDD: PSM and RDD estimate earnings gains from program_participation of roughly $9,500–$9,900, while IV and AIPW estimate gains of $13,000–$13,800. The confidence intervals of PSM ($8,182–$10,961) and IV ($10,261–$15,738) do not overlap, indicating a genuine conflict between these two methods.
Likely explanation: PSM estimates the average treatment effect among matched participants and relies on the assumption that all confounders are measured. IV identifies the effect only for 'compliers' (those induced into program_participation by the instrument), who may benefit more than the average participant. AIPW's higher estimate likely reflects its ability to adjust for both selection and outcome model misspecification. The divergence suggests there may be unmeasured factors driving selection into program_participation that PSM cannot fully account for.
DID VS DRDID: DiD and DR-DiD estimate earnings gains of only $2,018 and $3,545 respectively, both statistically indistinguishable from zero (p=0.47 and p=0.87), while other methods find large, significant effects.
Likely explanation: Both DiD and DR-DiD require a parallel trends assumption — that program_participation and comparison groups would have followed similar earnings trajectories absent the program. With only 30 observations and a panel of this size, these methods have very low statistical power and extremely wide confidence intervals (DR-DiD spans from -$38,500 to +$45,600), making it impossible to detect even large true effects. The estimates are not necessarily contradicting the others; they are simply too imprecise to be informative.
FIXED_EFFECTS: The fixed effects model returned an estimate of exactly $0.00 with zero-width confidence intervals and a null p-value, which is a degenerate result.
Likely explanation: This is a technical failure, not a substantive finding. With 30 entities and 30 observations, each entity likely appears only once in the panel, leaving no within-entity variation for the fixed effects estimator to exploit. This result should be entirely disregarded.

Robustness to Hidden Confounding

For the PSM estimate (~$9,600), an unmeasured confounder would need to be associated with both program_participation and earnings by a factor of at least 27 to fully explain away the result — an extraordinarily high bar that makes confounding an implausible explanation. For the IV estimate (~$13,000), the bar is still very high at a factor of 18.6. For the AIPW estimate (~$13,800), the bar is even more stringent at a factor of 61.8. By contrast, the DiD and DR-DiD estimates have E-values of 1.0, meaning even trivially small unmeasured confounders could explain them away — consistent with their statistical insignificance and unreliable precision in this small sample.

Caveats

  • The fixed_effects estimate of exactly $0.00 is a degenerate computational result, not a meaningful finding. It appears the panel structure does not contain repeated observations per entity, making within-estimator identification impossible. This result should be excluded from any decision-making.
  • DiD and DR-DiD are statistically non-significant and have extremely wide confidence intervals (DR-DiD CI spans over $84,000), almost certainly due to the very small sample of 30 observations. Their failure to detect an effect should not be interpreted as evidence that program_participation has no effect on earnings.
  • IV estimates the Local Average Treatment Effect (LATE) — the earnings gain specifically for individuals who were induced to participate by the instrument. If the instrument selects a particularly motivated or high-return subgroup, the IV estimate may overstate the effect for the average enrollee.
  • All estimates are based on a very small sample (n=30, with PSM matching only 14 pairs). Results should be treated as preliminary. Confidence intervals, even where narrow, may not fully capture uncertainty from model misspecification or sample-specific idiosyncrasies.

Bottom Line
